# HOLE AUDIT — every attack surface in the Turnbuchalla proposal
*Live-researched 2026-10-23. Ordered by severity: what kills the pitch if
unaddressed → what invites a bad committee moment → what is already
partially mitigated but needs a sharper answer. This is the
"what Birnberg, committee staff, and the landlord lobby will find" list.*

## TIER 1 — holes that can sink it if not answered

### H1. The clean-year refund guts the deposit's purpose
The deposit exists to secure damage claims for the WHOLE tenancy, not
year one. Returning principal after 12 months removes the tenant's
skin-in-the-game exactly when damage risk accumulates (wear, pets,
party years). The model prices damage claims at *current* behavior —
15% turnover × 25% × $1,200 — but post-refund, claims frequency almost
certainly rises. The landlord lobby's line writes itself: "you took my
damage security and made every other landlord pay for it."
**Needs:** either (a) claims frequency modeled at post-refund behavior
with an honest uplift, or (b) a retained-damage-bond slice (e.g.,
refund 50%, keep a smaller damage bond in escrow for the tenancy), or
(c) claims-history pricing sharp enough to do the deposit's deterrent
work. This is the deepest actuarial hole — an actuary will hit it
before paragraph three is finished.

### H2. The M1PR leg doesn't reach everyone
The credit only exists for credit-eligible households: ~300K claimed it
in TY2022 (avg $964 — not the $2,720 max), the 2024 M1-filing reform is
projected to add ~119K claimants, and roughly **1 in 3 eligible renters
never claim it at all** (MPR/NWH figure). Against ~600K renter
households, the "renter-side share" maybe reaches ~420–450K.
Over-$77,570 households have no credit to assign — they pay directly
(whitepaper admits this in drawback 11). Non-claimants gap silently.
**The claim "households pay nothing new out of pocket" is only true for
credit claimants.** Needs: an honest coverage-percentage, a non-filer
auto-enrollment answer, and a "who pays for the top-income renters"
line that doesn't hide behind drawback 11.

### H3. "Rides the existing CRP rail" overstates the plumbing
Verified: since TY2024 every landlord MUST e-file CRPs through DOR
e-Services — the *reporting* rail is real and mandatory. But that rail
moves **data**, not money. Redirecting an assigned share of a person's
refundable credit to a third-party insurance pool is **new machinery**
— a DOR build, new statute language, and probably a legal question
about assigning refundable credits (revenue recapture intercepts exist
for *debts*; an ongoing premium assignment is not a debt intercept).
Opposition framing is free: "they're taxing your tax refund."
**Needs:** reword to "rides the existing CRP *data* rail; the
assignment itself is a new DOR mechanism built on top of it" — honesty
here buys credibility everywhere else.

### H4. Experience-rated vs. community-rated — the docs contradict
Concept sheet: "premiums are experience-rated." Whitepaper §2:
"community-rated flat % of rent basis for rating the landlord share."
Those are opposites. The whitepaper's own rationale for flat rating is
anti-redlining — fine — but then "experience-rated" in the concept
sheet and fairness clauses is the version staff will quote back.
**Needs a decision:** the standard reconciliation is community-rated
*base* + experience-rated *surcharge* (claims-history modifier on top
of a flat floor). One sentence of drafting resolves it; leaving both
versions in the packet is a credibility wound.

### H5. "No recapture, ever" vs "repayment plan auto-attaches"
Whitepaper mechanism table: "mediation/repayment plan auto-attaches" to
a hardship claim. Concept sheet: "tenants owe no recapture." If a
tenant on a repayment plan pays back arrears, that IS recapture with
softer branding — or if the plan only covers the *ongoing* rent while
the claim covered arrears, say exactly that. An adverse reader
(HOME Line included — tenant-side reviewers are not friendly to
disguised recapture) will call it the subrogation clause in a costume.
**Needs:** precise language — what does the repayment plan repay, to
whom, and what happens on plan default?

### H6. The median landlord's math doesn't pencil — and they'll say so
The savings pitch (~$7,260 per avoided contested eviction) only helps
landlords who actually fight evictions. The modal landlord — small
portfolio, rare filings — pays ~$6/mo/unit matched share as a NEW
mandatory cost with no offsetting event. MN Multi Housing's line:
"a tax on the 90% who never needed it." The honest counter exists
(that's what insurance IS — and losing deposit float is a cost they
already absorb today) — but the packet currently argues averages, and
the lobby argues medians.
**Needs:** a "what the median landlord actually gets" paragraph —
claims plumbing, free adjudication, registry data, lower screening
cost, deposit-paperwork elimination — or concede the point and price
the small-landlord track lower.

## TIER 2 — committee-moment holes (won't sink it alone, will hurt)

### H7. Year-one float ramp is overstated
"Deposits fund year one" assumes the full ~$780M is escrowed on day
one. Migration runs over 12 months at lease renewal — average custody
in year one is roughly half, so year-one float ≈ ~$12M, not ~$23M.
Either name a seed appropriation/backstop or admit the premium ramps.

### H8. Cash-flow timing mismatch
M1PR is an ANNUAL credit paid at tax time. Premiums need monthly
revenue. The braid as written has a built-in 12-month liquidity gap —
somebody (state fiscal agent? carrier?) front-loads the year. Not
addressed anywhere in the whitepaper.

### H9. Is "verified hardship" insurable at all?
Insurance prices fortuitous events. "Job loss / medical crisis" is
closer to unemployment insurance — a benefit program — than a
traditional peril. This is THE regulator question: is home agency
Commerce (insurance law) or a benefit-administration frame? UI is the
friendly precedent (state-run, experience-rated, payroll-funded —
exactly this model's shape). Frame it that way *first*, before a staff
attorney asks "what's the covered peril?"

### H10. The SDR industry is an organized national opponent
"Renters Choice" is an active state-by-state legislative campaign by
Rhino/LeaseLock/Obligo (Cincinnati and Atlanta already passed
SDR-legitimizing laws). A mandatory public pool that outlaws their
product isn't just fighting the local landlord lobby — it draws a
funded national trade opponent. GOOD news in the same research:
Oregon (2021, 2022), Washington, and Philadelphia killed SDR bills in
committee, and Baltimore's mayor vetoed one — legislatures are already
suspicious of these products. And Sen. Blumenthal's Oct 8, 2026 letter
to the FTC on deposit alternatives gives the "why now" section a
federal tailwind. Use all of it.

### H11. Docket-diversion claim needs an honest fraction
"A large share of ~20–25K filings" is vague. The pool only diverts
nonpayment/hardship + deposit cases — holdover, lease breach, and
criminal conduct stay in court by design. Serial filings are ~30–45%
of filings (Eviction Lab) but not all serial filings are
hardship-triggered. Honest number is probably "roughly a third to
half" — which is still 7–12K cases a year and is a plenty-big number.
Claiming "removes them from the docket entirely" without scoping
which "them" invites the correction in committee.

### H12. Single-subject exposure
The bill spans insurance regulation + landlord-tenant statute + tax
credit mechanics. MN applies the liberal germaneness test (Johnson v.
Harrison) and comprehensive bills on one general subject survive —
"residential rental risk" is arguably one subject — but a challenger
gets a colorable argument for free. Mitigation is structural: keep the
credit-assignment mechanics inside a conforming section that reads as
machinery for the pool, not a separate tax policy.

### H13. Small-landlord track = adverse-selection lane
The <4-unit simplified track is a good politics fix that creates a bad
insurance hole: the riskiest, least-professional landlords self-sort
into the cheap lane. Needs either different pricing for the simplified
track or claims-history capture that follows the unit, not the
portfolio size.

## TIER 3 — already acknowledged but needs a sharper answer

- **Insurer insolvency/backstop** — mitigation listed, but the fiscal
  note will show a contingent state liability and committees sticker-
  shock on fiscal notes. Pre-draft the fiscal-note answer.
- **25% admin load adequacy** — ~$26.5M/yr for 40K claims/yr of
  adjudication plus ~600K escrow accounts. That's ~$660/claim for
  adjust-and-administer before the first escrow dollar moves. Plausible
  but thin — verification-heavy claims cost more than auto claims.
- **Claims-registry privacy** — per-owner published data vs FCRA-type
  accuracy/dispute rules (drawback 8 covers it; name the mechanism).
- **Sunset vs. investment horizon** — a 3-year sunset reassures
  skeptics but tells the administrator/carrier not to build for year 4.
  Consider 5-year + mandatory report instead of hard sunset.
- **Belongings leg** — duplicates private renters insurance the market
  already sells at ~$15/mo; opt-in vs mandatory still parked correctly.
- **Belongings + hardship interaction** — a fire that destroys
  belongings probably also destroys habitability; claims coordination
  between legs is undesigned.

## What the research CONFIRMED is solid

- CRP e-file mandate is real (DOR e-Services, mandatory since TY2024)
- Serial-filing data, CFPB rental-debt figures, Harris County cost model,
  Visale clawback — all verified last pass
- Opposition precedents cut FOR us: OR/WA/Philadelphia killed SDR bills;
  Baltimore vetoed; FTC rulemaking on rental fees proposed March 2026;
  Blumenthal letter Oct 8, 2026 — the timing argument is real
- NCLC report "Tenant Insecurity" (May 2026) and NHLP's SDR framework
  give ready-made endorsement language — NHLP's own fix-list (ban
  deposit+fee stacking, cap waiver fees, ban subrogation) is a subset
  of this bill
