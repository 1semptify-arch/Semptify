When your rights are violated, nothing arrives in the mail. No notice, no flag, no friendly letter saying what just happened to you was not allowed. The protection exists on paper — but paper doesn't knock on your door.

That is the quiet problem underneath most housing disputes. A repair goes unmade for months. A deposit comes back short, with no explanation. A complaint is answered with a notice to leave. Each of those may already be against the rules — renters frequently can't tell, because nobody's job is to tell them. The first time many people learn what their rights were is after they've already lost the chance to use them.

The bill being drafted on this site starts from a simple observation: a documented fact beats a loud argument. If the record of what happened — dates, photos, notices, responses — counted for something the moment it was made, a renter wouldn't have to discover their rights after the fact. The record would carry them there.

What it aims to change

The draft is early — an outline, honestly labeled. These are its aims, not promises:

  • Your record counts. Plain-language standards for how housing problems get documented — so that a photo with a date and a repair request with a timestamp carry real weight, the same weight for everyone.
  • Answers have a clock. Reasonable timelines for responses — on both sides. A renter who reports a problem deserves to know when to expect an answer; a housing provider deserves a clear ask, not an open-ended complaint.
  • Telling the truth is protected. Reporting a real problem shouldn't be a risk. The draft aims to protect honest reporting from being answered with punishment.
  • One standard, any size. The same documentation standard for a four-unit landlord and a four-hundred-unit operator — scaled so small providers aren't crushed by paperwork meant for the big ones. How to do that fairly is one of the open questions.

Who it changes things for

Renters get something they rarely have today: a record that was worth keeping. Not a promise that every dispute goes their way — a fair chance for their side to be heard.

Honest housing providers get the same thing, from the other direction. When a claim isn't true, a good record is what proves it. The standard that protects a tenant's real complaint is the same one that protects a provider from a false one. This bill is not aimed at anyone — it is aimed at the fog that hurts both sides.

Courts and mediators get better files. Most housing cases don't fail because someone is lying; they fail because nobody can show what actually happened. A shared standard means disputes arrive smaller and resolve faster.

And everyone watching gets something rarer than any of that: a law being drafted where they can see it. Every edit is public. Every question asked on this site is answered or honestly marked unanswered. Students can follow an idea from a first outline all the way to — if it earns it — a statute.

What it is not

It is not law. It is not filed. It is not before any legislature. It is an outline that a small, donation-funded organization is writing in the open, and it will change many times before it deserves a sponsor. It may never become law at all — and if it doesn't, the public record of how it was written still belongs to everyone.

What it can already do, today, is make the invisible visible — starting with the idea that your own record of your own home should count.

The hope, stated plainly

Most renters lose long before anyone calls it a loss. They lose the day they decide there's no point in writing it down. This bill exists to make that decision wrong — to make the record you keep the thing that carries you. That's the hope, and it's the kind you can verify: it's written down, in public, one honest draft at a time.
