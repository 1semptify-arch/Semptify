Why look

When a landlord or a collection company says you owe money, they can report it to the credit bureaus — the companies that keep files on everyone's borrowing. What's in those files can be wrong: a balance that's inflated, a debt that isn't yours, an account you don't recognize.

You don't have to pay anyone to see your own reports. The federal government authorizes one site for exactly this, and it works the same in every state.

What you need

Your Social Security number, date of birth, and your current and recent addresses — from memory. About 10 to 15 minutes at a computer or phone. And a place to save three files: your own device or your own cloud storage.

The six steps

  1. Go to AnnualCreditReport.com. Type the address yourself. It is the only site authorized by the federal government for the official reports — and reports from all three bureaus are available every week at no cost.
  2. Choose "Request your free credit reports." Fill in the identity form — name, date of birth, Social Security number, addresses. This is the only typing.
  3. Answer the security questions. If the site can't confirm you online, that's normal — thin files fail sometimes. Call 1-877-322-8228 instead. Same official system. Reports arrive by mail in about 15 days.
  4. Pull all three bureaus — Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion. The site serves them one at a time, so go back and do each one. They don't all carry the same information.
  5. Save each report as a PDF. On a computer: Ctrl+P (⌘+P on Mac) → "Save as PDF." On a phone: print or share → save as PDF. Name them so you can find them — credit-equifax.pdf, credit-experian.pdf, credit-transunion.pdf.
  6. Read each report for landlord and collector entries. Check the accounts and collections sections for the name of a landlord, a property manager, or a collection company.

What to look for

A collection account in a landlord's, property manager's, or collector's name. A balance that looks inflated. A debt you don't recognize. For anything you find, write down the name, the balance, the account number, and the date it was reported — and keep the saved PDFs. A dated record is what lets the details get checked later.

If something looks wrong

Once you dispute an item, the bureau has to investigate — that's the law (15 U.S.C. § 1681i). The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau publishes an official walkthrough for filing a dispute. This guide covers pulling and reading your reports only — disputing is a separate step. If a court date or a deadline is involved, talk to a licensed attorney or your local legal aid society first.

What to skip

Skip any site that asks for a card number, sells credit monitoring, or leads with your credit score. The official reports don't come with a score — and you don't need one for this. Look-alike sites exist; some show only one or two of the three bureaus and bill you for services you have to cancel.

On the report sites themselves: pull and save only. You don't sign up for anything and you don't start anything inside them.
